The four linksWhat each check protects

LinkIt fails when…Check question
Questionthe question is vague, or its answer could not change anythingAm I answering a question that matters?
Groundthe conclusion stands on something false or uncheckedWhat do I stand on, and do I know it is true?
Reasoninga step does not follow, or another explanation fits the same factsDoes the conclusion follow? What else could explain it?
So-whatthe conclusion changes nothing, or nothing could change itWhat does this change? What would change my mind?

Two layersWhat good thinking is made of

Paul and Elder separate the standards a piece of thinking should meet from the traits of a good thinker.Standards: Paul & Elder, The Miniature Guide to Critical Thinking: Concepts and Tools (4th ed., 2006), p. 12 (free excerpt). Their web page lists eight; the guide adds significance. Traits: "Valuable Intellectual Traits". A checklist can test standards directly. Traits show up only as behaviors, so the checklist asks for the behavior.

A 1990 expert-consensus study for the American Philosophical Association reached the same two layers: six core skills (interpretation, analysis, evaluation, inference, explanation, self-regulation) and a set of dispositions, such as being "honest in facing personal biases" and "willing to reconsider".Peter A. Facione, Critical Thinking: A Statement of Expert Consensus for Purposes of Educational Assessment and Instruction (the "Delphi Report", 1990), pp. 3 and 8.

LayerItemsHow to check
Standardsclarity, accuracy, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, logic, significance, fairnessRead the draft and test each one
Traitsintellectual humility, courage, empathy, autonomy, integrity, perseverance, confidence in reason, fair-mindednessLook for the behavior, e.g. "I wrote what would change my mind"

SourcesWhere each check comes from

  • The chain as a whole: an argument is sound only if its steps are valid and all its starting points are true. "Validity and Soundness", Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy. Carl Sagan: "every link in the chain must work (including the premise) — not just most of them." The Demon-Haunted World (1995), quoted in The Marginalian.
  • First principles: Aristotle, Physics I.1: we know a thing only when we have "carried our analysis as far as its simplest elements."
  • The elements inside the links: Paul & Elder, The Miniature Guide to Critical Thinking: Concepts and Tools (4th ed., 2006), p. 5 (free excerpt).
  • Question: George Pólya, How to Solve It (1945): "What is the unknown?"
  • Why you need a check at all: Richard Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science" (1974): "you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool."
  • Reasoning, rival explanations: T. C. Chamberlin, "The Method of Multiple Working Hypotheses" (Science, 1890): a favorite explanation becomes "a ruling theory" unless you keep rivals alive.
  • Think before you ask the AI: Lee et al., "The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking" (CHI 2025).
  • Ask the AI for critique, not approval: Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models" (2023).
