Who does what in Volvo's new driving stack

On 15 October 2026, Volvo Cars and NVIDIA announced that future Volvo cars will drive “from door to door” on three NVIDIA parts. Each part has its own job. After this lesson you can take any job in the system and say which part owns it.

What Volvo announced

“Eyes-off” means the driver may look away from the road. “Mind-off” means the driver does not need to watch the system at all. The release gives no date. Volvo's head of software told TechCrunch the first car will “debut within the decade.”2

The press release names the three parts in one sentence: “the Hyperion sensor and compute architecture, Halos OS, and Alpamayo Stack driving software.”1 Volvo adds its own “vehicle safety systems and engineering expertise.” The goal is “a gradual introduction of eyes-off and mind-off driving,” first in a next-generation Volvo car.1

The release says almost nothing about what each part does. For that we need NVIDIA's own pages. Here is the short version.

Three parts, three kinds of job

You already know robots, so start there. A robot has hardware (sensors and a computer), a base layer of software that keeps it safe, and a policy that decides what to do. Volvo's stack splits along the same lines.

Hyperion  the hardware: sensors and computers

TOPS means trillions of operations per second. INT8 means 8-bit whole-number math, a common format for running neural networks in cars. A reference set is a starting point: Volvo's software head said Volvo will “standardize more on Hyperion,” also “from a sensor set perspective.”2

NVIDIA calls Hyperion 10 “a reference production computer and sensor set architecture.”3 In plain words: a tested design for which sensors go on the car and which computers process their data. The reference set has 14 cameras, 9 radars, 1 lidar and 12 ultrasonic sensors, plus two DRIVE AGX Thor computers, each with “1,000 TOPS of INT8” compute.3 A carmaker that uses it does not have to design and test this hardware from zero.

Halos OS  the safety base: certified software and fixed rules

ISO 26262 is the main safety standard for car electronics and software. It has four risk levels, ASIL A (lowest) to ASIL D (highest). A hypervisor is software that splits one computer into walled-off parts, so a crash in one part cannot spread to another.

Halos OS is the software layer that keeps the car safe even when the AI is wrong. NVIDIA describes it in layers.4 Three of them matter for this lesson:

  • A protected core. Halos Core is “the next generation of NVIDIA DriveOS” and meets ISO 26262 ASIL D. Its hypervisor “isolates safety-critical functions so failures can't reach vehicle controls.”4
  • A classical stack. Halos OS “combines system services with classical perception and planning capabilities”:5 an engineered way to see and plan that does not depend on the AI driver.
  • Fixed rules. Halos Applications are “rule-based safety guardrails for AI.”6

Alpamayo  the AI driver

Alpamayo 1, the open research model: 4 cameras with 0.4 s of history go in; a 6.4 s path (64 points at 10 Hz) and reasoning text come out. About 10 billion parameters.8

Inside the car, NVIDIA describes Alpamayo as “an in-vehicle reasoning model that interprets road scenes and generates driving trajectories.”5 It is a vision-language-action (VLA) model, the same kind of model you know from robot policies. One difference: it also writes out its reasons in words. NVIDIA calls this text “Chain-of-Causation.”8

Volvo  the car and its own safety know-how

Volvo builds the car, joins the parts together, and brings its own safety systems and “nearly a century of real-world safety research.”1

Two stacks at the same time

The most important idea in this lesson is the pattern in Figure 1. NVIDIA's head of automotive, Ali Kani, said it simply: “The point is we run these two stacks at the same time.”2 NVIDIA uses the same design in the Mercedes-Benz CLA: “an AI end-to-end stack for core driving, alongside a parallel classical safety stack.”7

Why both? The AI driver is flexible but hard to prove safe. The classical stack and its fixed rules are less flexible but easier to test and certify. Running both gives a second, independent path when the AI is wrong.

Compare with your robot

Your pick-and-place design splits by phase: rule-based motion planning moves the arm close, then a VLA does the grasp. One part is in charge at any moment. Volvo's stack splits by role: the AI and the classical stack run at the same moment, and a checker decides what reaches the wheels. Keep this difference in mind. A later lesson asks what it would take to run such a checker during your grasp.

NVIDIA has already brought this design to robots. Halos for Robotics (June 2026) uses Halos OS as “the software stack for robotics safety” on IGX Thor.11

“Alpamayo” means two things

Older news says “NVIDIA DRIVE Hyperion.” NVIDIA renamed it “NVIDIA Hyperion” in September 2026.9 It is the same product.

News stories use one name for two different things:

  • The open Alpamayo models (Alpamayo 1, 1.5 and 2 Super) are large models for research and development. In NVIDIA's words: “Rather than running directly in-vehicle, Alpamayo models serve as large-scale teacher models that developers can fine-tune and distill into the backbones of their complete AV stacks.”9
  • The Alpamayo Stack is the software in the car. It contains a compact model that runs on DRIVE AGX Thor,5 plus the safety parts above.
