Lesson 1

The four links of thinking

About 12 minutesPlus one AI rally, today or tomorrow

Good thinking turns a question into a conclusion you can use. On the way, it passes through four links. Like any chain, it is only as strong as its weakest link.

By the end of this lesson you can:

  • name the four links: Question, Ground, Reasoning, So-what;
  • spot the weakest link in a short example;
  • start an AI rally that attacks your weakest link instead of praising you.

IdeaStart from what thinking is for

You asked what makes thinking good. Instead of collecting tips, let's reason it out from the purpose.

We think because we need to decide, explain, or understand something, and our time and attention are limited. So a piece of thinking is good when its conclusion is:

  1. about a question that matters,
  2. true enough to rely on, and
  3. useful: it changes what you do or believe.

The second requirement can fail in two different ways. You can start from something false, or you can take a step that does not follow. Logic has a word for an argument that avoids both failures: it is sound only when its steps are valid and all of its starting points are true."A deductive argument is sound if and only if it is both valid, and all of its premises are actually true." "Validity and Soundness", Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy. So three requirements give four links:

  1. QuestionAm I answering a question that matters?
  2. GroundWhat do I stand on, and do I know it is true?
  3. ReasoningDoes the conclusion follow? What else could explain it?
  4. So-whatWhat does this change? What would change my mind?

Ground means the facts and assumptions your conclusion stands on. One question per link is enough to start. Those four questions are your checklist, version 0.1.

One more rule follows from the chain: fix the weakest link first. Making a strong link stronger adds nothing. This is how you spend your brainpower where it matters.

IdeaHow this fits the classic answers

Richard Paul and Linda Elder of the Foundation for Critical Thinking answer your question in two layers:Paul & Elder, "Universal Intellectual Standards" and "Valuable Intellectual Traits", Foundation for Critical Thinking.

  • Standards of the thinking: clarity, accuracy, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, logic, significance, fairness. You can check these in a draft.
  • Traits of the thinker: intellectual humility, courage, empathy, autonomy, integrity, perseverance, confidence in reason, fair-mindedness. You cannot check a trait directly. You check the behavior it produces, such as "I wrote down what would change my mind."

They also list eight elements found in all reasoning. All eight fit into the four links:Paul & Elder, The Miniature Guide to Critical Thinking: Concepts and Tools, Foundation for Critical Thinking.

LinkPaul & Elder's elements of thought
QuestionPurpose; question at issue
GroundInformation; concepts; assumptions
ReasoningInterpretation and inference; point of view
So-whatImplications and consequences

So the four links are not a new theory. They compress tested frameworks into something small enough to hold in your head while you work.

That is why George Pólya's advice for solving any problem begins with "What is the unknown?", before any work on the answer.George Pólya, How to Solve It (Princeton University Press, 1945). The question list starts with "What is the unknown? What are the data? What is the condition?"

PracticeRecall, then diagnose

First, from memory: say the four links in order, with one check question each. Then open this.
  1. Question: Am I answering a question that matters?
  2. Ground: What do I stand on, and do I know it is true?
  3. Reasoning: Does the conclusion follow? What else could explain it?
  4. So-what: What does this change? What would change my mind?

For each case, pick the weakest link: the place where the thinking broke first.

You want to "catch up on robot foundation models". Two hours later you have 23 tabs open (papers, blogs, videos) and you cannot say what you learned.

A topic is not a question. "Could any of these models run on our robot arm this quarter?" would tell you which 20 tabs to close.

Your report says: "We can train the robot in simulation, so we will need far fewer real-world tests." Later you learn that skills learned in simulation often fail on the real robot.

The step is fine if skills carry over from simulation to reality. But that was an untested assumption, and it was wrong. The conclusion stood on weak ground.

Your 10-page summary of a new tool covers its history, five features and twelve use cases. Your manager reads it and asks: "So, should we use it?"

Lots of true content, but no conclusion that changes a decision. One so-what sentence at the top would have answered the manager.

You read three success stories about teams that adopted a new AI coding tool. You write: "This tool makes teams faster."

The stories may be true, so the ground is fine. But three successes do not show that the tool caused the speed. Other explanations: fast teams try new tools first, and failures are rarely written up.

You spend a day comparing two AI models' benchmark scores to the second decimal place. Your team will choose by price and license, and both models are already accurate enough.

The question is clear, but it does not matter: no answer to it can change the decision. Mattering (significance) is part of the Question link.

Your article ends: "In conclusion, this technology has both advantages and disadvantages, and only time will tell."

This sentence is true of every technology, so it says nothing. A real so-what takes a position and names what would change it.

Your plan is built on one line from a social media post: "Humanoid robots will cost less than a car by 2028." You never found out who said it or how they know.

The whole plan stands on one claim you have not checked. Ask: who says so, and how would they know?

After last week's software update, your robot's failed grasps doubled. You write: "The update broke grasping." In the same week, the lab also changed its lighting.

The facts are true: failures did double after the update. The weak step is "after it, so because of it". The new lighting could explain the same facts, and you did not rule it out.

Use itYour first AI rally

Why do you need a checklist and a partner at all? Because your own weak links are the hardest ones to see. Richard Feynman said it best:

"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself—and you are the easiest person to fool."
Richard Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science", Caltech commencement address, 1974

An AI can be that partner, but only if you set it up well. Two rules come from research:

  • Think first, then ask. In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, people with more confidence in AI reported less critical thinking, while people with more confidence in themselves reported more.Hao-Ping Lee et al., "The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking", CHI 2025, Microsoft Research. So you write your conclusion before the AI sees it.
  • Ask for critique, not approval. AI assistants tend to agree with the person they talk to, a habit called sycophancy.Mrinank Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models", Anthropic, 2023. So the prompt forbids praise and asks for one weak link at a time.

Pick one thing you are learning or writing about this week. Write your current conclusion in your own words. The prompt below updates as you type.

Paste it into your AI chat and play three rounds: answer its question, revise your conclusion, repeat. Doing this later today or tomorrow is fine. The gap helps you remember.

Read nextThe primary source

Richard Feynman, "Cargo Cult Science" (1974, about 15 minutes). A short, famous talk about the ways smart people fool themselves, and the habit that protects against it: reporting everything that could prove you wrong, not only what supports you. Read it with one question in mind: which of my four links would Feynman attack first?
