Seven findings

1. The AI cannot be the safety function

An independent, certified checker must limit what the machine can do. AI labs, chip makers, regulators, standards groups and safety researchers agree on this. DeepMind says "e-stops, barriers, and speed/force limits" "remain essential". Nine security agencies led by CISA say LLM-type AI "almost certainly should not be used to make safety decisions". The EU Machinery Regulation says a learning machine's control system "shall not cause the machinery or related product to perform actions beyond its defined task and movement space". Japan's AI Safety Institute notes that machine-safety principles require the safety-related part to be independent from everything else.DeepMind GR2 safety report p. 1; CISA et al. p. 9; Reg. (EU) 2023/1230 Annex III 1.2.1; J-AISI robotics guide v1.0 p. 28. → Lesson 1

2. Build layers, and know which one must hold

Use defense in depth across the five layers below. The line that must hold, even when the AI's computer is hacked, is the trust boundary between L3 and L4. Koopman's test: imagine the doer attacks "in the worst way possible, with full and complete knowledge of every aspect of the design." If harm is still possible, the checker is not independent enough.Koopman, Kane & Black (2019) p. 8.

3. AI brings new kinds of failure

Nothing needs to break for a learned policy to fail. It can choose an action whose meaning is unsafe, act on input unlike its training data, ignore its instruction, or point at objects that do not exist. In one study, VLA success fell "from 95% to below 30% under modest perturbations" such as a moved camera, and models tended "to ignore language instructions completely". DeepMind also found "a gap between semantically understanding a constraint (Text) and physically acting on it".LIBERO-Plus (2025, abstract); DeepMind GR2 safety report p. 3 (company-reported).

4. Security failures become safety failures

The camera's view is now an input channel. Researchers have hidden commands in signs placed in a robot's view, and printed patches cut VLA task success by "up to a 100% reduction" in simulation. Jailbreak attacks moved from chat to robots: RoboPAIR often reached "100% attack success rates", including "the first successful jailbreak of a deployed commercial robotic system". Backdoors can hide inside a policy with "near-100% attack success rates". Old-fashioned bugs remain too (see the threat model).CHAI (2025); Wang et al. (2024); RoboPAIR (2024); BadVLA (2025, preprint).

5. Testing alone cannot show safety

If a policy succeeds 50 times out of 50, you can only claim, with 95% confidence, that its failure rate is below about 6%. To claim below 1%, you need about 300 failure-free trials, run under the conditions of real use. Toyota Research Institute warns that "many robotics papers are measuring statistical noise". So combine testing with hazard analysis, fault injection, runtime monitoring and a safety case.Formula: n = ln(1 − C) / ln(R), used by RAND (2016); the arithmetic is ours. TRI, "A Careful Examination of Large Behavior Models" (2025, preprint).

6. The rules are changing, and dates matter for clients

  • EU: the Machinery Regulation applies from 20 January 2027. Under it, machine-learning safety components need third-party assessment, safety software must be protected against corruption, and safety decisions must be logged. Cyber Resilience Act reporting has applied since 11 September 2026: an early warning within 24 hours and a full notification within 72 hours. After the AI Omnibus, AI Act high-risk duties start on 2 December 2027 (Annex III systems) and 2 August 2028 (AI in regulated products).Commission: machinery; Commission: CRA reporting; Commission: AI Act.
  • Japan: machines that use physical AI are, in the labour ministry's words (our translation), "not currently subject to specific regulation" under safety law, and a review is under way. JIS still follows the 2011 edition of ISO 10218. Japan's AI Safety Institute published an AI robotics safety evaluation guide (v1.0, July 2026). It covers LLM, image and speech AI, and adds VLA models in the next edition. The Personal Information Protection Act was amended in July 2026, with new rules for biometric data such as face features; it takes effect within two years.PPC: 2026 amendment; MHLW interim summary (29 June 2026); JISC: JIS B 8433; J-AISI guide.

7. Read vendor claims with care

Model makers rule out safety-critical use of their own robot models. NVIDIA's "Halos Certified" lab is accredited as a second-party inspection body that reviews documentation. Third-party certification of the robotics stack is still in progress. Several physical-AI startups publish little or no safety engineering. Always ask who certified what, to which standard, at which level, and for which use. → Reading vendor claims, Lesson 7

Proving it is safe

A working demo is the start. To argue that a system is acceptably safe, the research describes these steps (taught in Lesson 6):

  1. Define the operating conditions (objects, lighting, people's access) and the safety bar for each hazard.
  2. Analyze hazards with more than one method: top-down (STPA) and bottom-up (FMEA). STPA assumes accidents can happen when components interact badly, "none of which may have failed".
  3. Design the architecture: non-AI safety functions that keep the AI-controlled equipment safe (ISO/IEC TR 5469).
  4. Assure the machine-learning part: requirements, data, training, verification and deployment (the AMLAS method).
  5. Verify and validate: test coverage, fault injection ("does the fault mitigation actually work"), and statistics.
  6. Write the safety case and have it assessed by someone independent of the team.
  7. Make a release decision with named people who accept any remaining risk.
  8. Operate and learn: monitor safety indicators, investigate incidents, and re-check the safety case after any retraining.

Sources: SACE v1.1 (University of York, 2026); STPA Handbook (MIT, 2018); AMLAS; UL 4600 voting draft (2019), which lists "use of a retrained neural network" as a trigger to re-analyze the safety case.

A minimal safety case for the running example

Written in the style of Goal Structuring Notation (GSN): a top claim, a strategy, and sub-claims that each point to evidence. This is a teaching sketch, not a complete case.

  • G1 (claim): The pick-and-place cell is acceptably safe for its task under its defined operating conditions.
    • Context: operating conditions (objects, lighting, work area, rules for people's access); acceptance criteria for each hazard.
    • S1 (strategy): argue over every hazard found by STPA and FMEA.
      • G2: In normal use, the VLA's grasp-hazard rate is below the target. Evidence: N failure-free trials and a 95% bound.
      • G3: Wrong or faulty VLA output cannot cause harm, because non-AI speed, force and zone limits override it. Evidence: fault-injection and hardware-in-the-loop reports.
      • G4: Input outside the operating conditions is detected and leads to a safe stop. Evidence: monitor validation with new objects.
      • G5: Foreseeable misuse and attacks are handled. Evidence: threat model and penetration test.
      • G6: Safety is maintained in operation; any retraining triggers an impact analysis. (Not yet developed.)

GSN elements: GSN Community Standard v3 (SCSC, 2021). Aurora's rule of thumb: "evidence without a claim is simply trivia and, conversely, a claim without evidence is baseless" (ESV 2023 paper).

Reading vendor claims: NVIDIA Halos as a case

What it is. NVIDIA Halos for Robotics "unifies safety elements from platform hardware and software across three layers": platform safety (the IGX Thor computer with a separate safety island, plus a sensor bridge), Halos OS (a safety operating system with applications such as the Outside-In Safety Blueprint), and ecosystem safety (the AI Systems Inspection Lab).NVIDIA Technical Blog (22 June 2026). The design follows the doer/checker pattern: an AI input monitor plus a decision maker on the isolated safety island.

The general lesson: a safety platform can help, but it does not make your client's cell safe or compliant. The integrator still owns the risk assessment, the safety functions of the cell, and the safety case. NVIDIA's own GR00T robot model card says the model "is not tested or intended for use in mission critical applications that require functional safety".

Questions for clients and vendors

A starting checklist for a review or assessment. Each question points to a layer.

  1. Which functions protect people? Which of them are safety-rated, to what level (PL or SIL), and certified by whom? (L4)
  2. If the AI computer were fully taken over, what would still stop the arm? (trust boundary)
  3. Can the AI, the planner, or anyone on the robot network change safety limits or settings? How is that prevented and logged? (L4, security)
  4. What is the robot's motion range, and what limits it: mechanical or electrical stoppers, or safety-rated limits? (L4, Japan)
  5. Under which objects, lighting and camera positions was the policy tested, with how many trials, and how were failures counted? (L2)
  6. What happens when input is unfamiliar, the camera freezes, or an instruction is ambiguous? (L1, L3)
  7. How are instructions checked before execution, and can a user's prompt reach the guardrail? (L1)
  8. Is ROS 2 / DDS security enabled and enforced? What network zones surround the cell? (security)
  9. How are model files and updates signed, and which file formats are loaded? (L2, security)
  10. What remote access, telemetry and cloud links exist, and who can command the robot? (security)
  11. Which standards and rules apply where the system runs, and who signs the technical file and declaration of conformity? (assurance)
  12. Is there a safety case, and who assessed it independently? (assurance)
  13. What triggers re-validation: retraining, new objects, software updates? (assurance)
  14. How are incidents and near misses recorded and reported? (assurance)
  15. Which data and models hold the client's know-how? Does the contract say who may use them, including fine-tuning and distillation? (data)
  16. Does any tool upload data or models by default, and to where? (data)
  17. What do the cameras record, for which purposes, and how were the workers told? (privacy)
  18. What happens when a remote link drops, and when it comes back? (L4, remote operation)
  19. How do vendors connect, and can a remote session reach the safety settings? (security)
