Lesson 7 About 20 minutes · Running example: an AI pick-and-place cell

Conventional Robots vs Physical AI

Clients often ask two questions: "How is this different from the robots we already have?" and "The vendor says it is safe. Is it?" This lesson answers both. It gives you a side-by-side comparison, and five questions that separate engineering evidence from marketing.

Your goal

Explain to a client what stays the same and what changes when a robot cell gets an AI brain, and judge a vendor's safety claim with five questions.

Warm-up: earlier lessons from memory

What stays the same

The hazard is the same: a moving arm with mass, speed and force, and places where a hand can be trapped. So machine safety still applies in full. The ISO 12100 order still holds: remove the hazard first, then protect, then inform users. Safety functions still protect people. Japan's rules still apply: the 80 W threshold, the fence rule (Art. 150-4), the motion range, the teaching and maintenance rules.

Japan's AI Safety Institute starts from the same place: machine-safety principles require "安全関連系を、それ以外の部分から独立させること" (keeping the safety-related part independent from the other parts).J-AISI, AIロボティクスに関するセーフティ評価観点ガイド v1.0 (July 2026), p. 28.

NVIDIA puts it in one sentence: "Traditional functional safety methods, built around deterministic software and deductive failure analysis, are necessary but no longer sufficient."NVIDIA Halos Safety Evaluation Framework (2026), conclusion. "Necessary" is the part clients forget. AI adds work; it does not remove any.

You already know this

This is like moving an application to the cloud. The old controls still matter (patching, access control, logging), and new ones are added for new failure modes (misconfigured storage, stolen keys). Nobody says "the cloud is secure, so we can drop patching". The same logic holds here: AI does not replace fences and safety functions.

Reading vendor claims: five questions

  1. Who checked it? First party (the vendor itself), second party (a body that is part of the vendor), or third party (independent)?
  2. Against what? Which standard, which level, which function. "IEC 61508 SIL 2 for the stop function" is a claim you can check. "Certified safe" is not.
  3. Done, or in progress? A certificate is evidence. A readiness inspection, a roadmap or a "when available" product is not, yet.
  4. What does their own fine print say? Model cards, safety notices and usage terms often say more than the marketing page.
  5. Who owns the rest? A certified part does not make the cell safe. The integrator still owns the risk assessment, the cell's safety functions and the safety case.

A worked case: NVIDIA Halos

QuestionWhat the evidence shows (October 2026)
1. Who checked it?NVIDIA's AI Systems Inspection Lab is accredited as a "TYPE C (SECOND PARTY) BODY": it is part of NVIDIA. (ANAB scope)
2. Against what?Its method is "review of documentation", against functional-safety standards such as IEC 61508 and ISO 13849-1. (same source)
3. Done or in progress?"TÜV Rheinland is inspecting NVIDIA IGX Thor, Halos OS and Holoscan Sensor Bridge": a readiness check, not yet a certificate. (NVIDIA, September 2026)
4. Fine printThe Outside-In Safety Blueprint: "No claims are made on compliance with any Functional Safety and Cybersecurity standards". NVIDIA's GR00T N1.6 model "is not tested or intended for use in mission critical applications that require functional safety". (notice, model card)
5. Who owns the rest?Deployment near people "requires your own independent, certified safety system and a complete safety assessment". The robot-arm standards ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066 do not appear in the Halos sources reviewed for this course.

None of this makes Halos bad engineering. Its design follows the doer/checker pattern from Lesson 1. The point is that the evidence supports a smaller claim than "certified safe physical AI", and your client needs to know exactly which claim.

Practice: evidence or claim?

Check yourself

A client's manager asks: "We already run conventional robots safely. What is really different with physical AI?" Answer in three sentences.

1. The hazard is the same moving arm, so everything you do today still applies: risk assessment, fences or safety functions, and Japan's robot rules.

2. What changes is where behavior comes from: it is learned from data, it can be wrong with nothing broken, it can be steered through the camera view or language, and it changes when the model is retrained.

3. So we add work, not remove it: statistical testing under written conditions, protection of data and models, controls on the new connections, and re-validation after every retraining, while the certified safety layer stays independent of the AI.

Read the primary source

NVIDIA, "Inside NVIDIA Halos for Robotics" (22 June 2026)

Read it with the five questions open, then read page 1 of the ANAB scope and the blueprint's safety notice. About 30 minutes.

Why this one: it is one of the sources you found yourself, and the clearest description of a vendor's full safety stack. Reading it next to the accreditation scope and the fine print is the best practice there is for reading vendor claims.
