Lesson 2 About 20 minutes · Running example: an AI pick-and-place cell

Threat-Model the Cell

You already know how to threat-model software. A robot cell adds new kinds of entry points, and an attack on it can end with a moving arm. This lesson gives you a map of eight entry points, a real case for each, and a way to sort controls into "extension of what you know" and "new for physical AI".

Your goal

For an AI robot cell, list its entry points, trace one attack chain from entry to physical harm, and name two controls: one that breaks the chain early, and one that still protects people at the end.

Warm-up: Lesson 1 from memory

Answer before you scroll back. Recalling something a day or two later is what makes it stick.

Same method, new map

STRIDE still works on a robot. The ROS 2 project published a full threat model of robot systems. It uses STRIDE to sort threats and DREAD to score them. One of its two case studies is a modular robot arm in an industrial pick-and-place application, very close to our running example.ROS 2 Robotic Systems Threat Model, Moulard et al. (written 2019, last modified 2021).

Three things are different in a physical AI cell:

  • The scene is an input. "Anyone who can put a sign in front of a camera can put text into the model's context." No network packet is needed.Exein, "Threat modeling physical AI" (10 July 2026).
  • Some attacks hide where code review cannot see them. A backdoor can live in a model's weights. The BadVLA study reached "near-100% attack success rates with minimal impact on clean task accuracy", so normal testing does not reveal it.BadVLA (2025, preprint).
  • Every chain can end in physical harm. So the last control must be one the attacker cannot reach: Lesson 1's L4. From January 2027, EU machinery law requires control systems to withstand "reasonably foreseeable malicious attempts from third parties leading to a hazardous situation".EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230, Annex III, 1.2.1.

Exein's advice is to "model the loop, not the components". Follow an attacker's input through the whole loop:

  1. Perception"the injection surface"
  2. Reasoning"the hijack surface"
  3. Action"where compromise becomes consequence"
  4. Memory and state"the persistence surface"
  5. Connectivity"the propagation surface"
You already know this

This is how you already think about an LLM agent with tools: an injection in the input, a hijacked decision, then a harmful tool call. In a robot, the tool call is a motion.

Eight entry points

Here is the running example as a data-flow diagram. The yellow circles mark the eight entry points this course uses. The dashed lines are trust boundaries: the cell's network zone, and the certified safety zone inside it.

Entry pointReal caseControl (layer)
E1 The sceneCHAI hid instructions in "misleading signs" in the camera's view. It was tested on drone, driving and tracking agents, and on a real robotic vehicle (2025). Printed patches cut VLA task success by "up to a 100% reduction" in simulation, and also worked physically (2024).Treat any text in view as untrusted (L1). Detect unusual input (L3). People stay protected by L4 whatever the camera sees.
E2 CommandsRoboPAIR jailbroke LLM-controlled robots, "often achieving 100% attack success rates". This included "the first successful jailbreak of a deployed commercial robotic system", a Unitree Go2 (2024).A guardrail outside the model (L1): RoboGuard cut unsafe plans "from over 92% to below 3%" (2025). "Implement authorization in downstream systems" (OWASP).
E3 Models and dataHidden backdoors in VLAs (BadVLA). PyTorch's torch.load(weights_only=True) allowed remote code execution in version 2.5.1 and earlier (CVE-2025-32434).Signed models, weights-only file formats, and control over who trains the model on which data (L2).
E4 Policy server linkLeRobot up to 0.5.1 used pickle.loads() on data "received over unauthenticated gRPC channels without TLS" (CVE-2026-25874, CVSS 9.3).Mutual TLS, no pickle, and the policy server kept inside the cell zone.
E5 Robot middlewareROS 2 security "has been turned off by default", and by default ROS 2 tries "to find all nodes on all hosts on the same subnet" (ROS 2 docs). One 2021 advisory listed 13 CVEs in six DDS implementations (CISA).Turn on and enforce SROS2 (ROS_SECURITY_ENABLE=true, ROS_SECURITY_STRATEGY=Enforce). Narrow discovery (ROS_AUTOMATIC_DISCOVERY_RANGE). Give each node only the topics it needs. Patch DDS.
E6 Wireless setupUniPwn on Unitree Go2, G1, H1 and B2 (2025): a key "hardcoded, same across all devices", a handshake that only checked for the string "unitree", and root commands through the Wi-Fi setup (CVE-2025-35027). "An infected robot can simply scan for other Unitree robots in BLE range and automatically compromise them."A unique key per device, strict input validation, and setup radios turned off when they are not in use.
E7 Cloud, fleet and remote accessUnitree Go1 had "an undocumented backdoor" that gave anyone with the right API key complete remote control (CVE-2025-2894). A Unitree G1 sent telemetry to remote servers "every 300 seconds without operator notice" (2025, preprint).List every vendor connection. Authorize per device. Push data out of the cell instead of letting outside systems reach in (CISA, p. 15).
E8 The safety functionsOn the Raven II surgical robot, researchers could "completely ignore or override command inputs from the surgeon" and abuse the emergency stop with "single packet" attacks (2015).Protect safety settings and signals against change, keep the safety network separate, and log every change. EU law asks for this (see below).

Trace one chain

A threat model is more than a list. For each entry point, trace the chain to harm and find two places to break it. Here is a chain through E4, using a flaw like the one in LeRobot:

  1. The attacker gets in (E4).A device on the factory network sends a crafted pickle message to the policy server. The server has no TLS and no authentication, so the attacker's code runs on it. Early breakMutual TLS, no pickle, and the server inside the cell zone. This stops everything that follows.
  2. The AI is now the attacker.The server sends back any actions the attacker wants. To the robot PC they look like normal VLA output, so L2 no longer means anything.
  3. Motion checks only help partly.L3 may reject wild commands, such as a target outside the work area. But the attacker can choose fast motions that stay inside L3's limits, through the area where a person stands.
  4. The certified layer still holds.The safety scanner sees the person, and the safety controller triggers a protective stop, whatever the commands say. Last lineL4 on separate, safety-rated hardware. This is the control you can prove to an assessor.

The early break is cheap and stops the whole chain. The last line is the one that must work when everything else has failed. A good threat model names both.

Practice 2: extension or new?

Sort each control. "Extension" means a normal IT or OT security program already does it, applied to the robot. "New" means physical AI needs something your current program does not have.

Summary: extension or new?

Extension of conventional securityNew for physical AI
Network zones and conduits around the cell (E4, E5, E7)The scene as an input channel that no firewall sees (E1)
Authentication and least privilege for nodes and remote access (E5, E7)Task checks outside the model, because jailbreaks reach the planner (E2)
Input validation, patching, unique keys per device (E5, E6)Backdoors in weights that code review cannot see (E3)
Safe loading and signing of model files (E3, E4)A certified safety layer that holds after a full takeover (L4)
Change control and logs for configuration (E8)Safety settings treated as the most valuable asset to protect (E8)

Some rows sit on both sides. E8 uses old tools, such as access control and change logs, to protect a new kind of asset.

Pick one entry point other than E4. In three or four steps, trace a chain from that entry point to physical harm. Then name the control that breaks it at the entry, and the control that still protects people at the end.

Example with E6 (wireless setup):

1. An attacker within Bluetooth range sends a crafted Wi-Fi name during setup, and the robot runs it as root. 2. The attacker now controls the robot PC, and the same exploit can spread to other robots in radio range. 3. The attacker sends motion commands; L1 to L3 run on the compromised computer, so they can be switched off. 4. L4 still triggers a protective stop when a person comes close.

Early break: a unique key per device, strict input validation, and setup radios off when not in use. Last line: L4 on separate, safety-rated hardware.

For client work

  • EU machinery law asks for these controls. From 20 January 2027, connecting another device, including "any remote device that communicates with the machinery", must not "lead to a hazardous situation". Software and data that are critical for meeting the safety requirements "shall be adequately protected against accidental or intentional corruption", and the machine must "collect evidence of a legitimate or illegitimate intervention" in its software or configuration.EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230, Annex III, 1.1.9.
  • Vulnerability reporting already applies. Since 11 September 2026, manufacturers covered by the EU Cyber Resilience Act must report actively exploited vulnerabilities: an early warning within 24 hours and a full notification within 72 hours.European Commission, CRA reporting.

ROS 2 Robotic Systems Threat Model

Read "Robotic Systems Threats Overview", then "Threat Analysis for the MARA Robotic Platform". About 30 minutes.

Why this one: it is a complete STRIDE and DREAD threat model of an industrial pick-and-place arm, written by the ROS 2 project. It was written before VLAs existed, so add E1 to E4 yourself while you read. In its own words: "Mitigating all attacks in this document does not ensure any robotic product is secure."
