Lesson 3 About 20 minutes · Running example: an AI pick-and-place cell

Control the AI's Permissions and Motion Range

An AI robot needs two kinds of limits. Permissions say what the AI may ask the robot to do. Motion limits say where and how fast the arm may move, whatever the AI asks. This lesson shows how to set both, who may change them, and what the standards and Japan's rules say about them.

Your goal

For an AI robot cell, write its permission limits and its motion limits. For each one, say which layer enforces it, who may change it, and which rule or standard supports it.

Warm-up: Lessons 1 and 2 from memory

Two kinds of limits

Permissions say what the AI may ask for: which tasks, which objects, which places, which tools it may call. They belong outside the model. OWASP's advice for AI agents: "Implement authorization in downstream systems rather than relying on an LLM to decide if an action is allowed or not."OWASP LLM06:2025 Excessive Agency. Exein says the same for robots: "enforcement must live below the model, in the kernel, the OS, or the hardware, where a prompt cannot reach."Exein, "Threat modeling physical AI" (10 July 2026).

Motion limits say where and how the arm may move, whatever the AI asks. L3 enforces them in software. L4 enforces them with certified safety functions. From January 2027, EU law requires that the control system of a learning machine "shall not cause the machinery or related product to perform actions beyond its defined task and movement space".EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230, Annex III, 1.2.1.

You already know this

Permissions are access control for the AI: an allowlist of actions with bounded parameters, its own identity, and no way to grant itself more. Motion limits are the sandbox it runs in. The difference: in a robot cell, the strongest part of the sandbox is certified hardware.

Permissions: what the AI may ask for

Here is a permission table for the running example. The planner asks for actions; something outside the model decides.

The planner asks for…DecisionEnforced where

Five rules make this work:

  • Default-deny. Give the AI only the actions the job needs, with bounded parameters. Exein: "Default-deny is the posture."
  • Check plans outside the model. RoboGuard grounds written rules in the robot's environment with a "root-of-trust LLM" that is shielded from user prompts, turns them into "temporal logic constraints", and checks each plan against them. Under jailbreak attacks, unsafe plans executed fell "from over 92% to below 3%".Ravichandran et al., "Safety Guardrails for LLM-Enabled Robots" (2025).
  • Refuse or ask when unsure. DeepMind's list of guardrails for robot agents includes "refusing tasks that violate operational constraints" and "requesting human help" when instructions are ambiguous.Google DeepMind, Gemini Robotics 2: Safety Evaluations (July 2026), p. 1.
  • A separate identity for the AI. "The agent needs its own identity: its own user, its own process boundary." Never root, never a shared service account.
  • No self-granting. The AI, and the people who chat with it, can never change the AI's permissions or any limit.

Motion limits: where and how the arm may move

Software limits (L3)

A work-area box, joint limits and speed caps in the motion stack. They catch most wrong commands early, and the robot can recover. They are not certified, and they run on the computer an attacker might control.

Safety-rated limits (L4) in ISO 10218:2025

The 2025 edition of the industrial-robot safety standard, ISO 10218-1/-2, makes these safety functions more explicit:Summary from Hartmann et al. (2026, preprint), which compares the 2011 and 2025 editions clause by clause. The standard itself is paywalled; check the text before you cite a clause to a client.

  • Monitored standstill (the new name for "safety-rated monitored stop"), speed monitoring, and limits on stopping time and stopping distance.
  • Software limits and dynamic motion limits (clauses 5.7.4–5.7.5), which replace the old term "safety-rated soft axis and space limiting".
  • Robot classes. Class I is a robot with "manipulator mass ≤ 10 kg, maximum force ≤ 50 N, and speed ≤ 250 mm/s"; anything bigger is Class II. Class II safety functions need PL d and SIL 2; Class I needs PL b or SIL 1.
  • Protected settings. Safety-function parameters are protected with checksums and identifiers (clause 5.3.5).
Key rule

No robot and no AI is "collaborative" on its own. The application is.

ISO 10218:2025 treats collaboration as "exclusively a property of the application, rather than the machine". The four collaborative modes from ISO/TS 15066 are now part of the standard: monitored standstill, hand guiding, speed and separation monitoring, and power and force limiting.Hartmann et al.; modes as listed in the DeepMind safety report, p. 12. The body-region force limits from ISO/TS 15066 now appear in ISO 10218-2:2025, Annex M (informative).

Japan's rules

  • Which robots. The industrial-robot rules do not apply to a machine whose drive motor is rated "八〇ワツト以下" (80 W or less). With several motors, the largest one counts.Ministry of Labour Notice No. 51 (1983).
  • The fence rule. Where contact with a running robot could endanger workers, the employer must provide "さく又は囲いを設ける等" (a fence, an enclosure or other measures).Ordinance on Industrial Safety and Health, Art. 150-4.
  • Two routes to work without a fence (2013 notice 基発1224第2号). (1) A risk assessment shows the danger is gone. It must look especially at the arm's force and kinetic energy, the chance of a person being trapped against surrounding structures, and the arm's shape. The results must be recorded and kept. (2) The robot is designed, made and installed to ISO 10218-1/-2:2011, and the designer, maker and installer each prepare a technical file and a declaration of conformity.MHLW leaflet (December 2013).
  • MHLW's examples of "no danger". Keep at least 500 mm between the arm and surrounding structures, or make sure a trapped person can be freed by hand without drive power. For force, the example counts only if the limit is guaranteed "制御によらず構造的に" (structurally, not by control). This is one example, not the only route, but it shows how cautious Japanese guidance is about limits that depend on software.
  • Standards status. JIS B 8433-1/-2:2015 still match the 2011 edition of ISO 10218; the 2025 edition has not yet been adopted as JIS.JISC database (checked October 2026).
  • PoCs count. A completed machine used on a trial basis at a production site is not "研究開発中のもの" (under research and development), so the rules apply to a PoC on a client's line.MHLW notice 基発第339号, 第3 Ⅰ 1(6).

Who may change the limits

A limit is only as strong as the process that changes it. For each limit, name its owner and its change control:

LimitLives inWho may change itControls
Task and object allowlistL1 guardrail settingsThe line or safety owner, through a reviewed changeVersion control, review, change log
Action bounds for the AI (work area, speeds)L3 motion stackEngineering, through code review and testsRe-test after every change
Safety-rated limits and protective fieldsL4 safety controllerOnly an authorized, trained safety personPassword or key, checksum, change log, re-validation
Teaching inside the motion rangeWork procedureWorkers with the required special educationWritten rules (including the arm's speed during teaching), a way to stop the robot at once, and a "work in progress" sign
Stoppers and guardsL5 hardwareMaintenance, following the procedureInspection

Teaching rules: Ordinance on Industrial Safety and Health, Art. 150-3. Special education for teaching work is at least 7 hours of classroom and 3 hours of practical training (安全衛生特別教育規程, Art. 18).

EU law adds record-keeping. The machine must "collect evidence of a legitimate or illegitimate intervention" in its software or configuration. The tracing log of safety software uploads must be kept "for five years after such upload". For machines with self-evolving behavior, data on safety decisions "is retained for one year after its collection".EU Machinery Regulation, Annex III, 1.1.9 and 1.2.1.

Practice: name the limit

Check yourself

For the running example, write two permission limits and two motion limits. For each one, say where it is enforced and who may change it.

Permission 1. The planner may only pick parts on the job's parts list and place them in bins A to C. Enforced by an L1 guardrail outside the model. Changed only by the line owner, through a reviewed change.

Permission 2. The AI has no action that changes speeds or safety settings. Nothing is exposed, so nobody can grant it through the chat.

Motion limit 1. VLA targets are clipped to the bin area. Enforced in the L3 motion stack. Changed by engineering, with tests.

Motion limit 2. A safety-rated speed limit and protective field near the operator's station. Enforced by the L4 safety controller. Changed only by an authorized safety person, with a checksum, a change log and re-validation.

Read the primary source

MHLW, 「産業用ロボットと人との協働作業が可能となる安全基準を明確化しました」 (December 2013)

Two pages, in Japanese. About 15 minutes.

Why this one: it is the official Japanese basis for working next to a robot without a fence, including the two routes, the examples of "no danger", and what a technical file must contain. Read it with the 2025 changes in mind, because it names the 2011 edition of ISO 10218. For the international side, the Hartmann et al. comparison is the best free summary of ISO 10218:2025.

"How does a risk assessment under route (1) work in practice?" · "What goes into a technical file?" · "How would the permission table look for an LLM planner with tool calls?"
