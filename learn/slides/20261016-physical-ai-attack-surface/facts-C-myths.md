# Facts C: boundaries. What the simple story gets wrong, what the systems do not do, what is contested

Collected 2026-10-16 for the article "where can an attacker get in when a learned model decides what a robot arm, an AMR or a car does, and which supplier controls can see each way in". Angle C only.

How to read the table
- Tags in notes: [factory] = robots/AMRs/production floor; [product] = shipped ADAS/ECU/vehicle functions; [supply-chain] = model, data, training service, pretrained weights; [general] = applies to all three.
- Quotes in notes were checked against text I extracted locally from the PDF or HTML, unless the note says "fetch-tool paraphrase".
- "arXiv vN" dates are the date of the version I read; first-posting date is given where it matters.
- WebSearch was refused (budget used up) at the start of this task, so every source was reached by a known URL, not by discovery. See "What I could not find" and "Access notes" at the end.

## Nearest alternatives to a learned model, per loop (one line each)

Sensing and perception
- Classical (rule-based) machine vision: a supplier picks it where parts and defects are well defined and a tolerance can be written down; the vendor's own framing is that deep learning is for variable surfaces and unknown defects (C161). Non-learning software also falls outside the Machinery Regulation's third-party assessment for ML safety components (C127).
- Safety-rated sensing on the safety path (speed, torque, zones) instead of a learned detector: a supplier picks it because the safety claim then rests on monitored physical quantities (C124).

Decision and planning
- Rule-based or formal planning (RSS-style rules, A*/pure-pursuit/DWB controllers, hard-coded fusion rules): chosen because it is white-box, easier to debug and interpret, and safety rules can be hard-coded (C117, C120, C162).
- Hard-coded actuator limits behind a learned planner (openpilot-style): chosen to bound how fast a wrong command can act (C130).

Command and motion
- Fixed program taught by teach pendant or direct teaching: chosen because the motion is known in advance, teach-mode speed is limited, and the safety validation is done against that motion (C159, C160, C124).

Reasons are the cited vendors' or authors' stated reasons where quoted, otherwise my inference from them; no source I read compares the cost of the alternatives.

## Which attacks are real now, which are near future (as the sources report)

- Demonstrated by independent researchers against shipped or production-grade ADAS functions: sticker/patch attacks on a 2021 vehicle's sign recognition (C107), fake-lane stickers on a Tesla (C110), phantom projections on Tesla and Mobileye 630 (C113). All were one-vehicle or few-vehicle demonstrations, mostly with the attacker knowing or reconstructing the model (C108, C115); no source I read reports an in-the-wild exploitation, and NIST's list of documented real-world evasion cases contains no vehicle or robot case (C109).
- Demonstrated only in simulation, on benchmarks, on datasets or at miniature scale: fusion-defeating objects (C118, C119), single-modality patches on fusion models (C121), lane-patch attack safety impact (C112), VLA patches and backdoors (C146, C147).
- Demonstrated on real research or commercial robots, with hedges: LLM jailbreaks of a Unitree Go2 and others (C143), printed-sign command hijacking of a small robotic vehicle (C145).
- Factory robot arms driven by a learned policy: the sources call this early. IFR expects wide adoption "over the next five to ten years" (C154); the open VLA reference model reaches under 90% on tested tasks (C157); VLA attack papers state such robots are "not yet deployed at scale" (C144). I found no demonstrated attack on a deployed factory robot driven by a learned policy.
- Learned perception inside shipped ADAS is already normal as far as vendors state (C151, C153), including end-to-end AI claims by Bosch; VLA layers are described as prepared, not shipped (C152).
- Supply chain: web-scale poisoning is called practical by its authors, with no known real-world case (C149); poisoning needs few documents for LLMs (C150); backdoors in VLA models are shown in simulation under outsourced training (C147).

## Facts that bear most directly on the article's question

1. C110 and C113: the demonstrated ways in for a shipped camera function (stickers on the road, light projected or shown on a billboard) need no network access, no root and no code change; Keen Lab says so for its two scenarios. A control that watches networks, ECUs or software integrity is not in that path (my inference from these two sources).
2. C101, C107, C108, C109, C114: whether a physical attack works depends on distance, angle, lighting, attack goal and attacker knowledge; "fails outside the lab" and "works on a real car" are both supported by the sources, so the article should state the conditions, not a verdict. C109 and C114 add that documented real-world evasion used simple methods, and that no vehicle or robot case is in NIST's list.
3. C117, C118, C119, C121, C122: redundancy and fusion defeat naive attacks and lose to attacks built for them; the demonstrations are white-box, simulated or miniature.
4. C124, C125, C126, C128, C130: the safety functions described check motion quantities (speed, torque, force, actuator limits, task and movement space) and the law defines them by risk of injury; I found no source in which a safety function judges whether the command, the scene reading or the part quality is correct (inference, absence in my reading). Their parameters can be changed by someone who reaches the controller (C125).
5. C127, C128: from 2027-01-14 the EU Machinery Regulation requires third-party assessment for ML that ensures a safety function and demands resistance to foreseeable malicious attempts; this is the main place where the law, not the engineer, forces a decision about learned models in a safety path.
6. C133, C141, C142, C140, C131, C132: the authors and agencies say prompt injection is not expected to be fully solved; the recommended control is a deterministic safeguard outside the model that limits what the system can do, and for robots that safeguard must be context-aware.
7. C136, C137, C138, C139, C140: robustness training and certification exist but cost accuracy, compute and generality; guarantees cover defined perturbation balls, not stickers, spoofed signals or text.
8. C148, C147, C149, C150: scanning of model files is not a poisoning check; poisoning and outsourced training are shown to be cheap or effective in LLM and simulated-VLA settings, with no known real-world case.
9. C154, C157, C144, C151, C152: the product side already ships learned perception, while learned-policy robot arms in production are described as 5-10 years from wide use; this decides which attacks are an inspection item now and which are a design item.
10. C114: the effort of a subtle ML-specific attack should be compared with simpler physical interference; the simple story that the attacker must use an adversarial patch is itself contested.
