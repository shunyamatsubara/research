Robot AI bets · Lesson 2 of 4 · about 15 minutes

The contenders

Seven ways to give a robot general skill, and the bottleneck each one bets on

Your win for this lesson

You can place any robot-AI company on one map by asking two questions, and you can say which bottleneck its approach assumes. That is the first thing to do when a client forwards you a new announcement.

In lesson 1 you saw Rhoda's bet: learn how the world moves from web video, imagine the next moments as video, then read actions off the imagined video. Rhoda is not alone, and it is not the only serious idea. Every company in this field is trying to solve the same problem: robot data is scarce and expensive, so the missing knowledge has to come from somewhere else.

Two questions that sort every company

  1. Where does the general knowledge come from? This is the main pretraining data: web text and images, web video, human video and wearables, or simulation.
  2. What does the model predict when it runs? Actions directly, the future (as video or as features) and then actions, or parts such as points and grasps that a classical planner turns into motion.

There is also a third fact that almost every family shares: they all finish with some real robot data. The bets differ on how much robot data is needed, and on what replaces the rest.

A useful way to read any approach: what does this company think is the real bottleneck? Their architecture is the answer to that belief.

Notice what the table says about your own work: in TiPToP's paper, grasping caused 31 of the 55 failures [6]. That is the same reason your pick-and-place design hands the final grasp to a learned policy.

Rhoda's closest rivals: other world action models

NVIDIA's survey of this family sorts “world action models” (WAMs) into three designs by how the actions come out [8]:

  1. Inverse dynamics: generate the future video, then a separate model infers the action that leads there. UniPi, VPP, mimic-video, LingBot-VA, Rhoda's DVA.
  2. Joint prediction: one model outputs video and actions together. GR-1, DreamZero, Cosmos Policy.
  3. Representation-only: trained with video, but generates no video when it runs. Fast-WAM.

Within design 1, Rhoda's choices are: full video generation at every step (Rhoda claims to be the first to run “full video denoising during real-time closed-loop robot control”; some rivals only partly generate the future, or skip it), a small separate inverse dynamics model, a causal model with a long memory (“hundreds of frames”), and pretraining from scratch on web video [9]. Like most rivals, it generates in a compressed latent space at low resolution, not in raw camera pixels [16].

The structural look-alike is the 1X World Model: a 14B video model plus a separate inverse dynamics model. But 1X reports it takes about 11 seconds to generate 5 seconds of video, so it plans in chunks instead of reacting [10]. And Fast-WAM challenges the core of design 1: it reached similar success at 190 ms per step, against 810 ms for its own imagine-then-act version [11]. Its claim: training with video helps, but generating video at run time may not be needed. If that holds, the most expensive part of Rhoda's loop is optional.

Rhoda has published no latency number. It says only that the loop runs “multiple times per second” [9]. This is the first number to ask for in a vendor meeting.

Three things to take away:

  • Video-backbone models often win on benchmarks and unseen tasks, usually with less robot data. This supports the family Rhoda belongs to — but not Rhoda itself, which has no published comparison.
  • VLAs are still several times faster and sometimes more robust. NVIDIA's survey puts full-video WAMs at a “3–4x slowdown” and says they “trade robot-data efficiency for compute cost” [8].
  • The lines are blurring. π0.7 can condition on subgoal images from a “lightweight world model” [15]; NVIDIA's next GR00T (N2) is a WAM built on DreamZero [8]; the same survey expects “WAM+VLA hybrids” to win. So a company's label tells you little. Ask the two questions instead.

Practice · retrieval, no scrolling up

1. A startup pretrains on 500,000 hours of data from cheap wearables worn by people, with no robot data in pretraining, then uses about one hour of robot data per task. Which family?

  1. Video world-action models
  2. Modular planning pipelines
  3. Human-data trained policies
  4. VLA action-head models
This is Generalist's GEN-1. The bet: real contact data is the bottleneck, and people wearing sensors are the cheapest way to collect it. Question 1 answers “human video + wearables”; question 2 answers “actions directly”.

2. A model is trained to predict future video, but when it runs it skips generating video and outputs actions in about 190 ms. Which family?

  1. Video world-action models
  2. Sim-data trained policies
  3. Latent world-feature models
  4. Human-data trained policies
This is Fast-WAM, the “representation-only” design of world action models. It uses video as a training signal only. It matters for Rhoda because it suggests the expensive generation step may not be needed at run time.

3. A model predicts future feature vectors, not pixels. It samples many candidate actions and keeps the one whose predicted features come closest to a goal image. Which family?

  1. VLA action-head models
  2. Modular planning pipelines
  3. Video world-action models
  4. Latent world-feature models
This is Meta's V-JEPA 2-AC. It shares Rhoda's belief that web video teaches physics, but rejects the idea of predicting pixels. The cost: about 16 seconds of planning per action, and it needs a goal image.

4. A system uses a vision-language model to point at objects, a grasp network to propose grasps, and a GPU task-and-motion planner. It needs no robot training data. Which family?

  1. Video world-action models
  2. Modular planning pipelines
  3. Human-data trained policies
  4. Sim-data trained policies
This is TiPToP. It beat π0.5-DROID (fine-tuned on 350 h) on 98 vs 55 of 165 trials. Most of its failures came from grasping, the weak spot of modular systems.

5. Which belief is the opposite of Rhoda's core bet?

  1. Robot teleoperation data is scarce and costly
  2. Models should predict the future before acting
  3. Web video pretraining is only a crutch
  4. Closed-loop control beats any open-loop video plan
Generalist's essay calls video pretraining another “crutch” and trains almost everything from scratch on real interaction data. Rhoda would agree with the first, second and fourth options.

6. A client says: “Video models beat VLAs now, so Rhoda must be ahead.” What is the strongest correction?

  1. The family wins some tests, but Rhoda published none
  2. Video models lose every single benchmark to VLA models
  3. Benchmarks never matter at all for factory robot deployment
  4. Rhoda's model is a VLA, so those results differ
Cosmos Policy, DreamZero and others beat π0.5 on several tests, but VLAs still win some robustness tests and are much faster. Rhoda has published no comparison with any VLA or other world action model. And Rhoda's model is a world action model, not a VLA.

A client asks: “Rhoda says internet video is the key. Generalist says video is a crutch. Which one should we bet on?” Write your answer in 3–4 sentences.

One good answer: Both agree that robot teleoperation data is the bottleneck; they disagree on what should replace it. Rhoda bets on web video for physics and still needs about 10–20 hours of robot data per task; Generalist bets on 500k+ hours of real contact data from wearables and claims about 1 hour per task. Neither claim has been checked by a third party, and Rhoda has published no speed numbers or baselines. So do not bet on a belief: run a pilot on your own task and compare success rate at full speed, recovery from errors, cycle time, and the robot-data hours each new task needs — and design the cell so the policy can be swapped later.

Name the three ways a world action model can produce actions, and put Rhoda in one of them.

(1) Inverse dynamics: generate future video, then a separate model infers the actions — Rhoda's DVA is here, with 1X and UniPi. (2) Joint: one model predicts video and actions together — DreamZero, Cosmos Policy. (3) Representation-only: trained with video but generates none at run time — Fast-WAM.

Primary source to read next

Moritz Reuss, “Pretrained to Imagine, Fine-Tuned to Act: The Rise of World Action Models”, NVIDIA Technical Blog, 15 June 2026. The best single survey of Rhoda's family: the three designs, speed and compute costs, and where DVA fits. It is written by NVIDIA, which builds its own world action models, so read its conclusions with that in mind. Read the sections on design types and on cost; skip the rest on a first pass.
